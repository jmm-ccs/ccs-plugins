# Fire, Smoke, and Odor Restoration Checklist

Use this reference for fire-loss claims. Adapted from Forensic Claim Analysis Checklists §7.

**This checklist is a starting point, not exhaustive.** Apply any additional reliable, independently-verifiable, industry-standard guidance for the specific fire conditions (smoke load, char depth, suppression-water pattern, contents impact). Use `WebSearch` to verify NADCA duct-cleaning scope, structural-engineer-letter requirements, and odor-neutralization techniques before citing them.

## Why this matters

Fire claims are the most multi-faceted residential losses — heat, acidic smoke, penetrating soot, *and* the suppression water from the fire department all interact. Initial carrier estimates are notoriously low because adjusters miscategorize severe smoke damage as a "cleaning" job rather than the structural replacement it usually is.

Almost every fire claim also has a water component. Apply `water.md` alongside this file.

## Items to audit for

### Smoke and soot — the hidden penetration

Smoke acts as a pressurized gas during a structural fire and drives acidic particles into hidden cavities. Standard cleaning will not get it out. Audit for:

- **HVAC ductwork**: full duct cleaning (NADCA-aligned scope), filter replacement, possibly coil cleaning, possibly duct replacement if porous flex duct is involved.
- **Electrical outlets and switches**: soot enters via outlet boxes. Each outlet/switch may need R&R and the box itself may need cleaning.
- **Insulation**: insulation absorbs smoke. Often requires removal and replacement, especially batt insulation in wall cavities.
- **Sealed wall cavities**: drilling, HEPA vacuuming, sealing.
- **Attic insulation**: blown-in insulation is a particularly bad smoke sink — supplement for full removal and replacement.

### Structural impact of heat

Wood framing exposed to fire may be:

- Lightly charred → can stay if structural engineer signs off
- Surface-charred → requires mechanical abrasion (sanding/blasting) plus a smoke-sealing primer (e.g., shellac-based BIN, KILZ Original, Fiberlock Aftershock)
- Deeply charred → must be sistered or replaced

A structural engineer's letter is typically required to differentiate.

### Specialized cleaning beyond "wipe down"

- Soda blasting / dry ice blasting / abrasive media blasting for charred framing (much more expensive than standard cleaning, often missed).
- Ultrasonic cleaning for contents (separate scope, often handled by a contents company).
- Chemical sponging vs. wet cleaning by surface type — the carrier's "clean" macro almost never differentiates.

### Odor neutralization

Basic ozone treatments are rarely sufficient for severe residential fires. Industry-standard advanced techniques:

- **Hydroxyl generation** — continuous, safe to run with occupants present
- **Thermal fogging** — penetrates same paths smoke took
- **Chemical encapsulation** — primer/sealer coats on framing, joists, subfloor
- **Activated carbon / scavenger pads** for stubborn pockets

If the carrier listed only ozone (or worse, "deodorize" with no detail), supplement aggressively with documentation of what's actually required.

### Suppression water

Fire department water enters via:

- The roof (hose streams through windows or through the roof itself)
- Doorways and broken windows
- Floors above the fire (firefighters' water cascades down)

Every fire claim with suppression water should also be audited against `water.md` — extraction, drying, demolition, antimicrobial, IICRC drying logs.

### Contents

- Pack-out for all contents in affected and smoke-impacted areas
- Off-site cleaning at a contents facility
- Storage during reconstruction
- Loss-of-use accommodations for occupants (separate insurance coverage but flag if missing)

### Total-loss vs. partial-loss decisions

If the structural fire damage exceeds ~50% of dwelling limits, the math may favor a teardown and rebuild rather than restoration. This is a strategic call with the user — do not unilaterally recommend it, but flag the threshold if you see it crossed.

## Common adjuster mistakes

1. "Clean walls / clean ceilings" listed once when the smoke load actually requires seal-and-paint on every framed cavity.
2. No HVAC duct cleaning or filter replacement.
3. No insulation removal in smoke-affected rooms.
4. Outlets/switches/cover plates not R&R'd.
5. Ozone listed as the only odor treatment.
6. Suppression water completely ignored — no drying logs, no extraction, no IICRC alignment.
7. Structural engineering omitted — adjuster decided on their own that framing was OK.

## Supporting evidence to require

- Pre-loss photos (if available — often from real estate listings or insurance underwriting photos)
- Post-loss photos by room and elevation
- Soot swab / wipe samples sent to lab (PLM or HVS testing)
- Thermal imaging of framing
- Structural engineer's report
- Fire department incident report (cause and origin)
- HVAC technician's inspection report

## Documented optimization example

In one case study cited in the source checklist, a carrier's initial estimate of $289,282 was supplemented to a final RCV of $435,913 — a delta of ~$146,000 — by identifying multi-layered damages, requiring structural engineering reinforcements, and applying O&P correctly across the multi-phase restoration. This is consistent with industry expectation on fire claims and grounds the scale of corrections to expect.
